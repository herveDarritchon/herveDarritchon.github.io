About (Engineering)

I started coding at 10. Early 80s. My first computer was a Tandy MC-10 — a real shock (in the best sense). I still remember my first BASIC lines and that moment when you hit Run… and something happens because you wrote it.

Then came my teenage years and the machines that shaped my taste: Atari ST, Amiga 500. The Amiga, especially, was a turning point: an OS, a terminal, tools, a “systems” mindset. That’s when development became a discipline, not a hobby.

What I’ve been doing since

I’ve spent more than two decades building and running software in demanding environments: first C/Unix systems (billing, maintenance, production support — the real world, the kind that trains your standards for “software that actually runs”), then Java/JVM platforms, software architecture, and today tech leadership on reliability and security topics.

The common thread hasn’t changed:

  • APIs as products: design, governance, tooling, and everything that prevents slow motion chaos.
  • Dev and Run: what matters is what happens after “it works on my machine”.
  • Pragmatic stacks: C, Java, Kotlin, JavaScript… not for collecting languages, but because constraints move and software must ship.
  • Technical leadership: unblocking, making decisions explicit, keeping systems coherent as they scale.

What I refuse to do

I don’t sell magic potions.

  • No “just adopt X and everything will be fine”.
  • No architecture cosplay: patterns without pressure, abstractions without proof.
  • No “speed” achieved by pushing debt onto the next team — or future you in 18 months.
  • No “best practices” thrown around like commandments.

When a choice has trade-offs (it always does), I’ll say it plainly: what you gain, what it costs, and what becomes harder later.

The problems I’m genuinely interested in

I’m at my best on problems where reality forces clarity:

  • Platforms and internal tooling: DX, guardrails, conventions that reduce chaos without killing autonomy.
  • API design and integration: contracts, versioning, compatibility, and edge cases that only show up in production.
  • Production-driven architecture: observability, failure modes, security constraints, operability, 2am support.
  • Refactoring living systems: reducing coupling, shrinking blast radius, paying down debt intentionally — without stopping delivery.
  • CI/CD and release discipline: making shipping a reliable process, not an event.

What you’ll find on this blog

I write here about what I use to keep projects readable and shippable:

  • architecture & modularity (what to split, what to keep together)
  • signal-driven refactoring (not mood-driven)
  • JVM (Kotlin/Java), CI/CD, and tooling that actually changes day-to-day work
  • AI as an engineering tool — and when not to use it
  • Hugo / editorial pipeline (to share knowledge simply)

Transmission: sharing and documenting

I also do the “less visible” but decisive part: reviews, documentation, mentoring, internal workshops, practice sharing — everything that prevents teams from relearning the same lessons the hard way.

Outside of work

  • I used to coach Cesta Punta — I’ll let you look that one up (timing and reflexes).
  • I’ve been practicing judo for a few years (discipline and consistency).
  • I maintain open-source modules around tabletop RPG tooling (Foundry VTT), because building tools is instructive (and it keeps your ego in check).
